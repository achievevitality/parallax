# The World Is Not Broken Into Sides

I want to begin with three ordinary problems.

A woman’s father is critically ill halfway around the world. The only hours when she can reliably speak with her family overlap with her job. Her employer needs her working. She needs the job. She also needs to remain connected to a family in crisis.

A woman is married to a man who is sick with cancer. She loves him. She also lives inside years of financial damage, anger, dependency, and consequences that keep landing on her and her family. Her faith tells her that care matters. Her remaining life matters too.

And imagine a practitioner working with a client who refuses the conventional medical option. The client’s autonomy is real. The medical risk is real. The practitioner’s professional limits are real. The client’s unmet need is real.

We know how to turn all three into arguments.

Family versus work.

Compassion versus boundaries.

Autonomy versus safety.

We are very good at sides.

And I think that may be one of the reasons we are in so much trouble.

Not because disagreement is bad.

Not because conflict is imaginary.

Not because everyone secretly wants the same thing.

But because we routinely manufacture conflict before we have adequately reconstructed the Reality in which the apparent conflict occurs.

We receive two representations. We instantiate two sides. Then we ask which side should win.

And by then, some of the most important information may already be gone.

Take the first woman.

At first glance, her family and her employer are competing for her time.

But look one layer deeper.

Her father needs care and connection. Her husband, who is physically present with him, needs a workable way to coordinate. She needs information, connection, income, and enough stability to continue functioning. Her employer needs the work she is responsible for to be reliably performed.

Almost everyone in that system benefits if she remains both employed and connected to her family.

The enormous conflict—job versus dying father—shrinks.

A real incompatibility may remain. One human being cannot give the same minute of attention completely to two things.

But now the conflict is where it actually lives: in particular minutes, particular tasks, particular communication requirements.

That is a solvable kind of problem.

The second woman gives us a harder example.

Cancer creates legitimate vulnerability. Care matters.

But illness does not automatically create a legitimate claim to unlimited access to someone else’s money, nervous system, labor, or remaining life.

And leaving a destructive arrangement is not causally identical to abandoning a human being.

Once we stop asking, “Should she stay or leave?” a much richer set of questions appears.

What care is actually required?

What responsibilities still belong to her husband?

Who carries the debt?

Who absorbs the consequences of his choices?

What does her faith actually require?

Which forms of support are compassionate, and which are maintaining the very system that is harming everyone inside it?

Some conflicts survive.

That matters.

A higher-dimensional view is not a machine for declaring that everything is harmonious.

It does something more useful.

**It allows incompatibility to remain exactly as local as Reality makes it—and no larger.**

I think we desperately need this capacity now.

Our institutions are built to simplify.

Medicine needs diagnostic categories. Law needs defined claims. Companies need roles. Governments need jurisdictions. Software needs objects. Social media needs content that can be classified, ranked, and routed quickly.

These simplifications are not stupid. They are necessary.

The problem begins when the representation we needed in order to act becomes the Reality we believe we are acting on.

Then a patient becomes a diagnosis.

An employee becomes a performance problem.

A political disagreement becomes two populations.

A safety concern becomes a reason to stop listening.

A person who refuses one form of care becomes a person who refuses care.

A rule created to protect people becomes incapable of perceiving the person standing in front of it.

**The map acquires jurisdiction over the territory.**

And because our systems are becoming more powerful, faster, and more interconnected, errors in representation propagate farther than they used to.

Artificial intelligence accelerates this problem dramatically.

An AI system can receive a partial representation, classify it almost instantly, retrieve an enormous body of knowledge associated with that classification, and produce an extraordinarily coherent answer.

The coherence can be breathtaking.

And completely downstream of the wrong object.

**More intelligence downstream does not repair corruption upstream.**

That is the problem I have spent much of my life circling.

For more than twenty years, I have worked across health, trauma, business, technology, spirituality, organizational design, and human development.

I kept finding the same strange pattern.

Something would look irrational until I could see the function it was serving.

Two people would look opposed until I could see the system producing the opposition.

A symptom would look like an error until I could see the requirement it was protecting.

A professional boundary would look obvious until a real human being arrived whose needs crossed six different professional maps at once.

And again and again, the most useful move was not to become more certain.

It was to increase resolution.

To ask:

**What is actually here?**

What functions are being carried?

What relationships connect them?

Where do the consequences go?

What information is missing?

What distinction have I collapsed?

What have I treated as an object because my current map did not have enough dimensions to represent the relationship?

There is a phrase I use for this:

**Reality gets the first word and the last word.**

The first word means we reconstruct before we adjudicate.

We do not begin with the answer and force Reality through it.

We do not begin with the institution’s categories and assume the person must fit them.

We do not begin with two sides simply because two sides have arrived at the microphone.

We look at the ground.

Then we build the best model we can.

And Reality gets the last word because our beautiful model still has to survive consequence.

If the bridge falls down, the bridge was wrong.

If the treatment makes the person worse, something needs reopening.

If the policy succeeds on its metric while exporting devastation somewhere the metric cannot see, the success is incomplete.

If an AI becomes increasingly eloquent while increasingly difficult for Reality to correct, its apparent intelligence may be becoming more dangerous rather than more trustworthy.

This is not an argument against expertise, rules, institutions, science, medicine, law, or artificial intelligence.

It is an argument against giving any finite representation sovereignty over the Reality it was created to help us navigate.

That distinction matters because we are entering a period in which human beings are acquiring extraordinary power to transform systems faster than we understand them.

AI can act across information systems at enormous speed.

Biotechnology can alter living systems.

Financial systems move consequences across the planet in milliseconds.

Organizations can coordinate millions of people.

Algorithms can reshape what entire populations notice.

Our transformative capacity is increasing faster than our ability to perceive the full causal ecology of what we transform.

**That is a dangerous asymmetry.**

And I do not think the answer is a smarter central authority.

No person can see the whole thing.

No discipline can see the whole thing.

No institution can see the whole thing.

No AI can see the whole thing.

Reality is too large, and every intelligence is finite.

The answer has to include differentiated intelligence.

Different people need to remain different.

Different disciplines need their specialized maps.

Different communities need local sensing.

Different intelligences need to be able to disagree.

But those differences need ways to remain coupled strongly enough that consequences can travel back to the systems producing them.

A doctor should be able to discover that the patient’s lived experience contains information the diagnostic model did not.

A company should be able to discover that the employee breaking a process has encountered a condition the process cannot represent.

An AI should be able to discover that the human rejecting its answer is not necessarily the noise in the system.

A society should be able to discover that two groups screaming contradictory truths may each be carrying information that cannot yet coexist inside the available model.

Sometimes one of them will simply be wrong.

Sometimes both will be wrong.

Sometimes there really will be an irreducible conflict and a painful decision will have to be made.

But **we should earn the conflict before we enthrone it.**

That requires a different kind of epistemic infrastructure.

Systems in which uncertainty can remain visible long enough to teach us something.

Systems in which disagreement does not automatically require domination.

Systems in which a person can say, “Your model does not fit what I am experiencing,” without being forced either to submit to the model or reject the entire institution.

Systems in which local intelligence can act without becoming disconnected from wider consequence.

Systems in which the ability to correct the system grows alongside the system’s power.

Because power without corrigibility is not intelligence maturing.

**It is error acquiring leverage.**

This is where I think our civilization has a choice.

We can keep building systems that become more capable by becoming more centralized, more certain, more optimized, more surveilled, and more difficult to contradict.

That path can produce extraordinary short-term performance.

It can also produce systems that fail beautifully—coherently, efficiently, and at scale.

Or we can learn to build systems in which greater capability comes with greater permeability to consequential Reality.

That is harder.

It means expertise without false sovereignty.

Coordination without forced sameness.

Safety without blindness.

Autonomy without isolation.

Boundaries without pretending that what lies across the boundary is irrelevant.

It means designing environments in which being corrected is not a failure of intelligence.

**It is one of intelligence’s primary functions.**

There is a line I have come to trust:

**Reality can correct the instrument before it can inform it.**

Sometimes we know our model is wrong before we know why.

A patient says, “That explanation does not fit.”

A scientist gets the anomalous result.

An employee says, “This process is failing here.”

A community refuses the intervention designed for its benefit.

An AI loses confidence for a reason it cannot yet represent.

Those moments are not always evidence that the system should change.

But they are precious.

They are places where Reality may be knocking on a door our ontology has not built yet.

We need systems capable of leaving that door unopened long enough to find out.

Because the world is not fucked because humans are stupid.

The world is not fucked because we disagree.

And the world is not fucked because differentiation was a mistake.

**We are in trouble because our power to act through simplified representations is outrunning our capacity to reopen those representations when Reality disagrees.**

That is a solvable problem.

Not an easy one.

Not a single technology.

Not a new ideology everyone needs to adopt.

It is a developmental problem.

We need to become better at building people, institutions, technologies, and relationships that can remain coherent without becoming closed.

That can hold a center without turning every direction they face into the center.

That can preserve distinctions without turning difference into Otherness.

That can act decisively while keeping enough of the model uncommitted for Reality to disagree with it.

And that can increase power while increasing the routes through which consequence can correct power.

If we can do that, the future does not require everyone to share one worldview.

In fact, it requires the opposite.

It requires a world full of different kinds of intelligence, carrying different maps, embedded in different places, capable of seeing things the others cannot.

And capable of remaining in relationship long enough to discover what none of them could see alone.

The task is not to construct the perfect map of Reality.

It is to build a civilization that can keep discovering where its maps are wrong.

Because Reality will always be larger.

And if our systems can remain open enough for Reality to correct them—

then greater intelligence does not have to mean greater control.

Greater coordination does not have to mean greater conformity.

Greater safety does not have to mean less freedom.

And greater power does not have to mean that our mistakes become impossible to stop.

We can build differently.

But first, we have to stop asking which side of the map is right—

long enough to look at the ground.
